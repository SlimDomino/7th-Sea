# Session 03 – The Pattern and the Padre

Played: Monday, October 19, 2026 (about 1 hour 40 minutes, on Discord)
In-game: the night of Julius 6, 1669, through the eve of María José's quinceañera on Julius 10
Place: Lute's ship, the NachtKarneval, in Altamira harbor, and the streets and markets of Altamira

Cast
- Captain Lute – Jessie
- Rafi (Rafael) Torres y Soldano – Joey (had to leave partway through)
- María José Soldano y Torres – Melissa
- GM – Gabriel (Esme, Sancho, Lute's crew, a very bad priest and everyone else)


## Previously

The ambush in the warehouse street nearly killed them. María José brought Lute's crew running just in time, the fugitive is safely aboard Captain Ocaña's ship, and Lute tore a glowing blood-red jewel off the neck of the man who led the attack.


## Aboard the NachtKarneval

Lute's crew hauls everyone back to the ship. Once the bandages are on and the adrenaline wears off, the damage is clearer: four Dramatic Wounds for Lute and three for Rafi. Bad, but not quite as bad as it felt in the street.

Lute can't stop looking at the jewel. It's a faceted oval ruby about the size of a thumbnail, and it seems to swirl when you look at it directly, threads of black and gold twisting slowly through the red, as if it isn't cut from stone at all but holds real blood moving inside a gem-shaped vessel. Lute sends Rodney to fetch Esme. Rodney salutes, has no idea what he's doing, and comes back half-dragging her, explaining that he had to tell her it was a Syrneth artifact to get her to come. Esme snatches it, taps it, puts it on, closes her eyes, takes it off, turns it over, and at one point licks it. Then she hands it back. "That's not Syrneth."

Lute puts it on. Where it lies against his chest it grows warm, and its dim glow begins to pulse in time with his heartbeat. Rafi hands over the rum. Lute, who doesn't drink, takes one mouthful, sprays it straight back out, and still gets half a buzz from what soaked in.

María José decides to stay aboard and help look after her brother and his captain. Sancho thinks this is deeply improper, and also thinks riding home at night during Los Borrachos with the Inquisition about is a worse idea. The compromise: she sleeps in Esme's cabin, and Sancho sleeps sitting against the outside of the door, so he can swear to her honor and her virtue. Everyone gets bandaged, fed and, except for Lute, plied with rum. Rafi drinks Lute's share.


## Lute's Dream

That night Lute dreams, and he knows the feeling: the same heaviness as the visions Malveck sent him on L'Il du Bête, the sense that someone else has chosen where he's going. A jungle, hot and loud with insects. A platform of grey stone set into the jungle floor, swept clean, as if the trees had been told to keep away. Lines cut into it, curves and loops and long sweeping arcs, glowing faintly blue-white, like moonlight under water.

He wakes in his cabin. The lamp is low, the harbor slaps the hull, and the jewel on his chest is glowing. He picks it up to study it.

And then he's walking. There's no ship, only a path under his feet, and every step is somewhere he's already been. Luckau, in the foothills of the Drachenberg, pine and cold air and woodsmoke, the luthier down the lane tapping a soundboard and listening. He's small, and his boots are too big. The yard behind the house, where a younger Rudolph says "Again," and the boy throws another uppercut with a Dracheneisen gauntlet far too big for him, and this time his father nods once. That nod was worth more than anything he ever said. His mother Maria in the kitchen, up to her wrists in dough, looking up at the boy in the doorway with eyes that see far too much, as if she's looking straight past him to the man he'll become, and isn't surprised by any of it.

Then the path drops into the dark under the trees, and it's cold, and Lute knows this night. The one he has spent years not remembering. The Red Cap is in front of him again, small and crooked, smelling of iron, its cap wet with something that isn't rain, grinning with too many teeth. Waiting for him to break, or run, or beg.

He doesn't. "I will not fear you." He holds its eyes for what might be moments or months or years, and a strong Resolve roll holds with him. At last it laughs, low and wet, almost pleased, the way you'd laugh at a dog that won't drop the bone, and the dark folds up around it.

Then the kitchen at night, one candle, and his father across the table asking gently what happened in the woods. This time the dream lets him answer as a man. He tells Rudolph that he stared down evil in those woods, that he'll live his life his own way whatever it costs, and that one day he'll bring that creature's hat home to him. Rudolph says nothing. He pulls up his sleeve and shows his son a scar Lute has never seen before, white and ragged, as if something bit him and didn't let go. He pours himself a drink, and his hand shakes. "You didn't run. Most grown men would have run. That's why I gave you the hand. Not for fighting men."

Then Lute does something he has never done. His father deserves to know everything, so he shows him: he calls up the forbidden sorcery, and the bandana knotted at Rudolph's throat crumbles to dust and drifts down onto the table. His father's eyes go wide. "You've grown far beyond what I expected."

The candle gutters out, and he's walking again, and then rising, up and up until he can see the path from above. It was never a path. It's the platform in the jungle, and he has been walking its lines all along, every memory a point on one design that winds inward in great loops and spirals. He steps into the center, and blue-white fire roars up along every line he has walked, no heat in it, only light and a sound like a great bell left ringing.

He comes apart into sparks, then comes back together looking out through red. Through the jewel. From very high up he sees Altamira lit for the festival, smoke from the fireworks drifting over the rooftops, and his own ship small as a toy in the harbor with one lamp burning in the stern window. And the sky isn't empty. Off to one side an enormous serpent writhes and knots around itself, its eyes the same blood red as the stone. Or its eye: one socket is empty. Across the heavens from it stands a unicorn, white, burning with a light so pure it hurts to look at. And between them hangs a wheel of light, like a wagon wheel, turning and turning and going nowhere.

Then the cabin rushes up to meet him, and he wakes for real.


## The Morning After

Full morning light, gulls, someone swearing on the dock, and the jewel still warm on his chest. No hangover. No pain. Lute looks down and finds that every bullet wound and sword cut is gone. In their place is thick, knotted scar tissue that looks diseased: angry red flesh shot through with threads of gold and black. He has no Dramatic Wounds left at all.

Lute's conclusion is that somebody slipped him something. He rounds up Rafi, Pickles, Esme and everyone else who matters into Rafi's cabin, takes his shirt off and shows them all. There is no healing magic in this world, as far as he knows. Is he possessed? Rafi, delighted, declares they've been healed by ghosts, and then wants to know why Lute has been holding out on him.

When Lute picks the jewel up, he gets a brief flash of the pattern he walked, and he understands: he is attuned to it now, and he can heal someone else with it. So he puts his hand over his best friend's face and spends a Drama Die. A red glow runs down his arm, through his hand and into Rafi, and Rafi drops unconscious.

For nine hours. And for all nine his face is twisted in agony, as if he's in terrible pain. He thrashes so hard that Lute, Rodney and another of the crew have to tie him to his bed. Through it all they can see his wounds knitting closed, and thin worm-like threads of red, black and gold working their way through the new scars.

Lute does not take this well. He retreats to his cabin, curls up in a corner, and is not okay for a while.

The crew, meanwhile, got properly introduced. Among them: Captain Pickles, the master gunner, who runs the gun deck in full clown costume and makeup and still can't work out why everyone calls him Captain. Rodney, the three-quarter mate. And the half-mate, Benoît, a Montaigne nobleman and former enemy captain who joined the crew rather than die when the NachtKarneval beat his ship. The crew has a nickname for him that won't be repeated here.


## María José's Visit

María José spends the morning deep in her journal. Later she knocks on Lute's door. There's no change in Rafi, she tells him, and it gladdens her heart that her brother has a friend like him. Lute swears he'll do anything to protect Rafi. What happened last night was unforgivable, and when the time comes he may need her family's courage. She tells him plainly where she stands: she follows the words of Theus, not so much the mouthpieces of the Church. And she has to go home and prepare for her quinceañera, her fifteenth birthday, on Julius 10.

Lute vanishes into his cabin and comes back with five hundred guilders. Happy birthday. He insists.

She absolutely cannot accept. There are traditions and meanings behind gifts, a whole social interplay, and this would not be seemly at all. Lute turns to Sancho instead. Sancho asks the señorita to let him speak with the captain alone, and then explains, very patiently: there are customs. A gift of gold is far too much. If the captain were to attend the quinceañera and bring a gift, not of gold, but of equivalent value... Lute takes the money back, and María José thanks him for his tact and graciousness. She and Sancho ride home to the hacienda.


## Shopping

Lute goes into Altamira to find a present. His first idea is a very fine doll. The best he can find is Montaigne-made: porcelain head and hands, painted with real gold and silver, lapis lazuli eyes, real human hair. Lute fails his Wits roll badly and starts to think the doll is a lovely idea, which is when the table points out the problem. Castille is at war with Montaigne, and a quinceañera is the day a girl leaves childhood behind. A Montaigne doll would have been a social bomb.

A second Wits roll comes up 13, and Lute finds two much better options: an Eisen spyglass and a Luckau violin. His hometown is famous for its tonewood, and for the luthier who makes only about one violin a year. The Montaigne dealer wants 800 guilders and launches into a speech about its magical tone. Lute slaps down the 800, takes the case and storms out, calling the man a Montaigne-brained son of a bitch in Eisen so as not to give offense. He didn't tip.

The next couple of days are a blur of dress fittings and last-minute details for María José. Lute spends his days smoldering, in the mood of a man about to go to war, and commissions two outfits from a seamstress who clearly thinks he's very strange and is more than happy to take his money. One is his Dusk outfit, ready in about five days. The other, rushed, is for the quinceañera: something like a gothic, vampiric take on mariachi. The bill is 150 guilders. Lute pays 300, then comes back an hour later with another 25. Everyone in Altamira who sells Lute anything now wants him to come back tomorrow.

(Lute is like this with everyone. He pays his crew far above the going rate and splits every haul with the whole ship, whether they were on the job or not, which is why they're forever being handed money and wondering why.)

Rafi finally wakes in the late afternoon, very freaked out, with the same gold-and-black scars as Lute. He's had a dream of his own, and he'll tell it himself next time. All we'll say is that it involves Tratch'can, Feragan and Jonah Coins.


## The Padre in the Fountain

Heading back to the ship from the seamstress, Lute crosses the square in front of the old fish market. It's still packed with the tail end of last night's revelry. And in the middle of the square, knee-deep in the big stone fountain, stands a priest. He has a wine bottle in one hand and his hat in the other, and he's scooping up water and flinging it over a delighted crowd. "In nomine Theus!" Splash. "And you, my daughter!" Splash. "And you, my son, you look like you need it most of all!" People are cheering, throwing coins, kneeling. Several of the women are laughing far too hard. He's big, sunburnt, handsome in an older, battered sort of way, very drunk, and the happiest man Lute has seen all week.

He spots Lute. "My son! You have an honest face! And an iron hand! Theus himself has sent you to me!"

Then five enormous men in bloodstained butchers' aprons come shoving through the crowd, and the one in front is sobbing. The priest was in his house. In his wife's room. At midnight. The priest explains cheerfully that he was only hearing her confession. Confession is private, my son. That's the whole point.

Lute speaks no Castillian to speak of, so he does the sensible thing: he grabs the priest by the collar and drags him off through the crowd, yelling at him furiously in Eisen so everyone can see how angry he is. The priest whispers that Lute should throw them some coins; he would, but he spent his last on this bottle. Lute flings about twenty guilders over his shoulder, and the butchers stop to pick it up.

Then a thin man in black pushes through the crowd with two large church wardens, holding a sealed letter in both hands. By order of His Excellency the Bishop of Altamira, Padre Baltasar Ruiz de Olmedo is directed to present himself at the monastery of San Bruno, there to observe five years of silent penance and reflection. "Do I look like a man who can go five years without speaking? Please, my son. You must help me."

"Welcome aboard, Bart." Lute informs the bishop's man that nobody takes one of his crew without proper paperwork. That may or may not be how the law works, but the priest and the secretary argue it out in rapid-fire Castillian, and the secretary finally nods and leaves. Padre Bart has bought himself a week before he has to report to the monastery.

Back on the ship, Lute makes his offer. The padre would be there to hear confessions and absolve sins, with a fair cut like everyone else. Lute pays well, and the penalties are steep for anyone who steals from him or can't control himself aboard his ship. There are three major rules: no kids, no animals and nothing without consent.

The padre has terms of his own. A cabin, not a hammock, with a door. A share like any other man aboard. Wine with dinner. And nobody ever asks him about the women. In return: "I'll bury your dead, I'll marry your fools, I'll bless your guns, and I'll hear the confession of any man who wants to make one. And I'll never tell a soul what I hear. Not the Bishop, not the Hierophant, not the Inquisition. Not even you, El Capitán."

Rodney loves him immediately. Captain Pickles drags him straight off to bless the guns. Benoît, as a Montaigne nobleman, is appalled by everything about him.


## Where We Left It

The NachtKarneval has a chaplain. Lute and Rafi are healed, and scarred. Lute has a Luckau violin and a party outfit on order. And the big day has arrived: Julius 10, María José's quinceañera. With Joey gone for the night, the party waits for next session, because it isn't the only thing that's going to happen.

- **Rafi's dream.** Tratch'can, Feragan and Jonah Coins. He'll tell it next session.
- **Rudolph.** Lute's father has a scar of his own, and now, at least in the dream, he knows about the Zerstörung.
- **Padre Bart.** He has a week before San Bruno expects him. The Bishop of Altamira knows exactly where he went.
- **The two henchmen and Inés.** We left the street without settling what happened to the wounded leader, the second henchman, or Inés, who still needs to get aboard Ocaña's ship before it sails.
- **The quinceañera.** Julius 10. María José turns fifteen, Lute has 800 guilders' worth of violin, and the whole province is coming.
