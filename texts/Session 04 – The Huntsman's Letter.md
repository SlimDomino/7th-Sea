# Session 04 – The Huntsman's Letter

Played: Tuesday, November 17, 2026 (about 50 minutes, on Discord)
In-game: Julius 10, 1669, the day of María José's quinceañera
Place: Lute's ship, the NachtKarneval, in Altamira harbor, and the Hacienda de los Robles

Cast
- Captain Lute – Jessie
- Rafi (Rafael) Torres y Soldano – Joey
- María José Soldano y Torres – Melissa
- GM – Gabriel (Gomez, Don Emilio, the guests and everyone else)


## Previously

The jewel healed Lute and Rafi, and left them both with strange gold-and-black scars. Lute found a Luckau violin for María José's birthday and a priest in a fountain, and the NachtKarneval now has a chaplain. The big day has arrived.


## A Letter from an Old Huntsman

As María José and Sancho head for the gangway to ride home, they find old Gomez standing at the top of the plank with a stranger: a man in the dusty brown habit of a lay brother, who has clearly been on the road a long time. He hands over a letter. Gomez reads the outside, turns, sees the captain, and passes it to Lute.

It's from de Huet, the Warden of L'Il du Bête, and it's written by a man who knows other people will read it:

"My friends of the carnival, forgive the coward's grammar. Letters travel, and mine will be read by more eyes than yours. Understand what I do not say."

The hunting season at his estate "ended badly." The master of the house was not pleased to learn his guests had left early, and has said so, loudly, to anyone who would listen. De Huet has been called a thief, a deserter and a drunkard ("two of the three are slander"). His house is gone, his pension is gone, and worse, his hounds are gone. But he writes from a cell with a good roof, three meals and a door that locks from the inside. "Our silver-haired friend" has put his name in front of people who matter, and nobody has hit him yet. Men in black have asked him more than once who his dinner guests were. He told them he never saw anyone. "I am a poor liar, but a stubborn one."

Then the real news. By the time the letter arrives, they'll have heard the bells. The cardinals could agree on nothing until they found a man nobody dislikes, and after a lifetime in the woods, de Huet says, that is the most dangerous animal there is. The doors that were shut for three years are open now because of them, and not one soul at the celebrations will ever know it. "That is as it should be. I find I do not mind."

"I said I would have to decide what kind of man I am. I have decided. I am the kind who writes the letter. If you are ever in Vaticine City and need a roof, ask at the pilgrims' gate for the old huntsman."

Signed, D. Huet.

Lute sums it up for Rafi: when they broke the archbishops out, de Huet was branded a traitor and lost everything. Now he's lying low in Vaticine City under the Church's protection, the freed archbishops gave the cardinals the numbers they needed, there's a new Hierophant, and if the crew is ever in town they have a friend.


## Before the Party

María José has one thing to do once she's home, birthday or no birthday: tell her mother about the bridge. On the festival's first night, Hermano Fidel tore a page out of his book at the checkpoint and sent a boy running into the city with it. Somebody needs to know about that, and Isabel will know who.

Before she goes, Lute pulls her aside with a question. He describes the Montaigne doll he nearly bought her, in all its porcelain-and-real-hair glory. Would that have been a gift she wanted? María José answers carefully: on the day a girl passes from childhood to womanhood, a doll would not be the most appropriate gift, and a Montaigne one, politically, would have been very much not so good. It would have been accepted in the spirit in which it was given, and treasured for that, but it would not have been her first choice. Lute is happy beyond his years that he didn't buy her such a travesty.

Lute collects his party clothes from the seamstress. He looks like he plays in the mariachi band, if the mariachi band really liked Dracula: black, gold and red. Rafi has gone the other way, all sunshine: white and gold, with lapis lazuli and red stones, and a cloak of peacock feathers.


## The Quinceañera

The Hacienda de los Robles has been transformed. Garlands and ribbons hang from every balcony and archway, hundreds of candles and lanterns light the courtyard, and the long tables are loaded with roast meats, bread, cheese, fruit, more wine than anyone could drink in a week and, of course, cheesy potatoes. Castillian guitars, clapping, dancing. Nobles from the neighboring ranchos, merchants, old friends and half the people Don Emilio has met in a lifetime of travel.

Juan was buried five days ago, and the house is still in mourning, but Isabel insisted the party go ahead, and go ahead properly. Life goes on in this house, and the whole province is watching to see whether it does. So the mourning is there, but quiet: black ribbons tied among the bright ones, a black band on every family sleeve, and at the head table, between Don Emilio's chair and Ana's, one empty place with a single white candle burning in front of it.

María José, in blue and cream, stands at the center of it all, and does what she always does: she watches. First her parents. Her father is completely in his element. Her mother stands a little to one side, talking to whoever comes up to her, and never once stops watching the crowd.


## The Entrance

About an hour in, when everyone has a drink in hand, Lute and Rafi arrive, with Rodney and Captain Pickles in tow. All four are in matching colors. Pickles, naturally, is dressed as a Castillian mariachi clown.

They do not simply walk in. Fireworks go up behind them, the music changes, and the four of them launch into a full dance routine that Pickles and Rodney have been drilling religiously for weeks, since long before anyone knew there would be a party to use it at. Their footwork is on point. Lute is quite sure he can keep up. Somewhere in the middle of it Rodney's shirt comes off. The guests will be talking about it for a long time.


## The Gifts

Once the dancing is over and the courtyard has stopped staring, Lute draws María José aside and hands her the black leather case. "I hope this isn't too much." Inside is the Luckau violin: tonewood from the village where Lute grew up, made by an old man famous across Théah who turns out one violin a year or two. He tells her to put it to good use, and that one day, when she travels, she can play it.

María José is deeply moved. He has given her not only a present but a goal, she tells him. She looks forward to the day she can play this beautiful instrument. She doesn't think she will ever match its beauty, but she hopes to do it justice, and to play for him one day.

When his turn comes, Rafi gives his sister a cloak of peacock feathers to match his own, and a small gilded box with an inscription on the lid. Inside is a set of engraved pistols, small and ladylike and easy to hide. Adventure is in the blood, he tells her, and these will keep her protected on her travels. A violin to keep her company and a pair of pistols to see her through.

"Mi hermano, you could not have given me a better gift."

Lute, having handed over his present, turns and starts drifting back toward the edge of the party, which for Lute is real progress.


## The Toast and the Dances

A glass chimes. Don Emilio is standing on his chair with his glass raised, and the music stops. It takes the courtyard a moment to quiet down. He waits for it, smiling, the way he's waited for rooms to fall silent his whole life.

"Friends. Neighbors. Family. And whoever that is by the wine keg, who I'm fairly sure I've never met. Welcome."

Then, before anything else, he raises his glass to the empty chair. "To my son. To Juan." The whole courtyard drinks, and for a moment the only sound is the candles.

"And now, because he would throw something at me if I made this a sad night: to María José. Fifteen years old. The cleverest person in this house, and she knows it, and she has the good manners never to say so. Mija, may you always get exactly what you want. And may God help the rest of us when you do."

Then he claims a father's privilege: the first dance. For one slow dance he is just a father, not a host, and a far better dancer than anyone would guess. When it ends he kisses her forehead. More than one ambitious neighbor brought a son tonight, hoping, but Don Emilio has made it known, kindly and without exception, that this year his daughter dances only with her family. Nobody argues, with Juan's candle burning at the head table.

So her brothers take their turns. Hernando, stiff and careful, as if she might break. Cristóbal, who dances the way he fences, perfectly and without a single wasted step, and doesn't smile until the very end. Father Pedro, surprisingly light on his feet for a priest.

And Rafi, who makes it a proper flamenco. While they dance he tells her the truth as he sees it: things are going to get dangerous for them again, so they might as well enjoy this life while they can. Celebrate, enjoy the night, because nobody knows what tomorrow brings. María José is just glad he made it home in time for her quinceañera. She thinks tomorrow will bring many wonderful things, and many more tomorrows after that. They drink to it.

Then she asks about his captain. He seemed upset, and he looked as if he was leaving. Did she upset him? Rafi assures her it's nothing she did, and takes the chance to make a joke at Lute's expense that Lute is lucky not to hear.


## Overheard

Lute, standing by the tables with his panzerhand on and a plate of cheesy potatoes, hears two guests talking behind him. A woman: "Have you seen it in the harbor? The striped sails? The acrobats on the deck? My nephew says the crew are all thieves."

The talk drifts on. Someone else says that Mateo Herrera's boy was let out of an Inquisition cell this week. Let out! Nobody gets let out.

Lute hears only the first part. He makes his way to Rafi as he comes off the dance floor. "Rafi, may I have a word?"

Rafi is outraged. He runs that show. Nobody on that ship is a thief except him and Lute, and the rest of them work for a living. (Whether "thieves" is technically accurate is beside the point. Nobody is supposed to say it out loud, least of all at his sister's party.) His first instinct is that they have to kill them. Lute's is to talk first: find out who's saying it and get to the bottom of it. Rafi's second idea is better. They have a whole ship full of performers, and they are at a party. Send Pickles or Rodney back to the NachtKarneval to round up the crew and put on an impromptu show, and prove they're real performers, not thieves. And make sure whoever goes tells the crew not to steal anything.

María José has one concern: the house is in mourning, Juan's candle is burning at the head table, and is a crew of loose-living sailors really the best thing to bring to her quinceañera? Rafi is sure Juan would want them to celebrate life. And Juan would not have taken kindly to anyone insulting his little brother.

Lute talked Rafi out of starting a dance fight with the gossips on the spot. The question of a show is still open. Nobody has given a thought yet to Mateo Herrera's boy.


## Where We Left It

The party is in full swing. María José is fifteen, with a violin, a pair of pistols and a peacock-feather cloak. Lute and Rafi have been insulted by a stranger's nephew, and are debating whether to answer with an acrobatic performance or something worse. The night is far from over.
